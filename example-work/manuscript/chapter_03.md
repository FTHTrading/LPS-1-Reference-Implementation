# Chapter 3: Greed Starts Whispering

Every developer has a number. 

Kevan sat back in his ergonomic chair, watching the spreadsheets recalculate. Six thousand kilograms of gold. Even at a conservative fifteen percent "sovereign discount" due to the logistics of Douala transit, the math was intoxicating. 

Four hundred million dollars of physical reserves, locked in a smart contract. 

With that kind of collateral backing the Unykorn tokenization protocol, the entire system would become instantly unkillable. The regulatory arguments would evaporate overnight. You couldn't call a token an unregistered security if it was backed by six tonnes of audited, vault-locked bullion. The valuation of Unykorn would double, then triple. Kevan was already mentally mapping the API integrations for the real-time reserve oracle.

Then the first invoice arrived on WhatsApp.

It wasn't a standard invoice. It was a poorly formatted PDF labeled `NKAMWA_CLEARANCE_FEE_REQ.pdf`, accompanied by a message from Prince Divine that broke the spell.

`"My brother, the gold is ready for transfer to the port. But the Douala customs office is demanding the activation fee for the transit permits. It is only forty-seven thousand dollars. A small thing for a king like you. Once the permit is active, the Brinks convoy rolls, and the bank releases the SBLC. Please wire the funds to the UBA Lusaka account listed below so we can close the stack."`

Kevan stared at the forty-seven thousand dollar figure. 

Forty-seven grand was rounding error in a four hundred million dollar deal. That was the magic of the advance-fee scam. The fee was always calibrated to look completely trivial compared to the payout. If you've already spent hours reviewing beautiful bank drafts and dreaming of a valuation bump, your brain desperately wants to believe that forty-seven thousand dollars is the only hurdle left.

"Only forty-seven thousand," Kevan muttered. "The classic entry ticket to the circus."

He looked at the banking details: *Nkamwa Divine & General Dealers, United Bank for Africa (UBA), Lusaka, Zambia.*

Kevan closed the spreadsheet. The greed that had been whispering in his ear for the last hour suddenly went quiet, replaced by the cold, analytical focus of a security engineer. 

He didn't open his banking app. Instead, he opened his OSINT toolkit, spun up a virtual private network, and initiated a search on the Zambian PACRA corporate registry. 

"Let's see what kind of 'Divine General Dealers' we are dealing with."
